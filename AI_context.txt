Currently, each trajectory is split into (N-window+1) subtrajectories, where N is the number of samples in the given traj and window is the observation window size.

Since the linear model is trained to learn perturbations, the initial first `window` samples  of the trajectory depict a causal relationship between speed & angle control and lidar residuals, where the angle=0 command means no residuals whatsoever. However, at the nth subtrajectory, the angle=0 command still might produce time-varying lidar, since the cars isn't perfectly oriented forward. Therefore , the learned linearized dynamics would differ from sample to sample.

Worse even : the speed of the car physically acts like a multiplicative constant for the speed of variation, whereas in the linear model  , it can only add things. Therefore, it's meaningless to train on arbitrary speed. It's better to organize training data in the following way :

- make 1000 csvs each containing `window` samples , instead of 10 csvs each containing N samples

- make sure that each csv has the same iniitial condition where the car faces forward

- only the angle is perturbed

- speed is constant

when the parameters get identified, the controller can then multiply the entire result of predicted residuals by the speed command value. If training data uses speed=1, then in webots simulation, setting speed=2 shall make the controller understand that the predicted residuals have to be multiplied by 2.


Regarding modeling of obstacles, the max lidar horizon could be reduced from 12000 to the max between lidar[-30] and lidar[30] at initial conditions (t=0) for each trajectory. Then when an obstacle appears in the horizon, the model would learn to apply a command proportional to the residual distance at a given angle.

Or a circular shaped wall could be placed at a high enough distance for the model to never crash into it when being subjected to arbitrary control commands.


Those have been some ideas about obstacle modeling.
